Work–Life Tipping Point

Two feedback loops decide whether a workload is sustainable. The diagram shows the loops. The model below shows where the balance tips.

Causal loop diagram

+ means the two variables move in the same direction. − means they move in opposite directions. The thicker loop is the one in control at the current slider settings. The limit on hours sits outside both loops. Its link is thickened when the limit stops the balancing loop and work builds up.

B: Work harder (balancing)

More work outstanding leads to more hours worked. More hours complete more work, and the outstanding work falls. This loop keeps the workload under control, but only up to the limit on the hours a person will work.

R: Burnout (reinforcing)

More hours worked leave less personal time. Energy falls, then productivity. Less work is completed, so outstanding work grows and hours rise again. This loop feeds itself.

Find your tipping point

Move the three sliders. The balance holds while the balancing loop can absorb the workload. It tips when the reinforcing loop takes over, or when the hours limit is reached and work builds up.

Your limit, whether set by law, contract or stamina. You extend your hours up to this limit to finish the work, and no further.
The size of the new work that comes in every week, measured in hours of effort. It is not the hours you end up working. Those are shown in the chart and rise when you are tired or behind.
Hours each week for family, rest, exercise and interests.

Assumptions: 70 hours a week remain after sleep and essentials. You work only the hours needed, up to your limit. Unfinished work carries over. Energy follows personal time with a delay of about a month. The model is illustrative and is not a measure of health.
